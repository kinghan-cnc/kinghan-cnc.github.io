Material Certifications for CNC Parts: What You Need to Know (MTR, RoHS, REACH)

February 22, 2026 · 11 min read
← Back to Blog

When you order CNC machined parts, you're not just buying shapes — you're buying verified material properties. The difference between a certified 316L stainless steel medical component and an unverified "stainless steel" part could be the difference between a successful device and a catastrophic failure.

Material certifications provide documented proof that the raw material used in your parts meets specified chemical, mechanical, and regulatory requirements. This guide covers the essential certifications every buyer should understand: Mill Test Reports (MTRs), RoHS, REACH, conflict minerals compliance, and industry-specific requirements.

Mill Test Reports (MTR / CMTR)

What Is an MTR?

A Mill Test Report (MTR), also known as a Certified Mill Test Report (CMTR) or Material Test Certificate, is a document issued by the material manufacturer (the mill or foundry) that certifies the actual chemical composition and mechanical properties of a specific production lot (heat) of metal.

An MTR typically includes:

  • Heat number: A unique identifier linking the material to a specific melting batch
  • Chemical analysis: Actual percentages of each alloying element (e.g., Cr, Ni, Mo, C for stainless steel)
  • Mechanical properties: Tensile strength, yield strength, elongation, hardness (as tested)
  • Material specification: The standard the material conforms to (e.g., ASTM A276, AMS 5643, JIS G4303)
  • Product form and dimensions: Bar, sheet, tube, etc., with sizes
  • Mill identification: Name, location, and certification of the producing mill

EN 10204 Certification Levels

The European standard EN 10204 defines different levels of material inspection documents. Understanding these levels helps you specify the right certification:

TypeNameWho IssuesDescription
2.1Declaration of complianceManufacturerStatement that material meets order requirements, no test results
2.2Test reportManufacturerIncludes test results from non-specific inspection
3.1Inspection certificateManufacturer's QA (independent of production)Test results from specific inspection of delivered lot
3.2Inspection certificateManufacturer's QA + authorized inspectorHighest level — verified by independent third party

For most precision CNC applications, EN 10204 3.1 certification is the standard requirement. It provides lot-specific test results verified by the mill's quality department. The 3.2 certificate, requiring a third-party inspector present at testing, is typically reserved for nuclear, critical pressure vessel, and military applications.

Why MTRs Matter for CNC Parts

  • Verify alloy identity: Confirm that "316L" is actually 316L and not 316 or 304. The difference in molybdenum content (2–3% in 316 vs. none in 304) determines corrosion resistance.
  • Ensure mechanical properties: A part designed for a specific tensile strength needs material that actually meets that strength specification.
  • Enable traceability: Heat numbers create a chain from finished part back to raw material origin — essential for recalls and failure analysis.
  • Regulatory compliance: Industries like medical devices (FDA), aerospace (FAA/EASA), and nuclear (NRC) require documented material traceability.

RoHS Compliance for CNC Parts

What Is RoHS?

The Restriction of Hazardous Substances (RoHS) directive is an EU regulation that restricts the use of specific hazardous materials in electrical and electronic equipment (EEE). The current version (RoHS 3, EU Directive 2015/863) restricts 10 substances:

SubstanceMaximum ConcentrationRelevance to CNC Parts
Lead (Pb)0.1% (1000 ppm)Free-machining alloys (12L14, leaded brass), solder
Mercury (Hg)0.1%Rarely relevant to machined parts
Cadmium (Cd)0.01% (100 ppm)Some plating processes, older brazing alloys
Hexavalent chromium (Cr6+)0.1%Chromate conversion coatings, some passivation
PBB0.1%Not relevant to metal parts
PBDE0.1%Not relevant to metal parts
DEHP, BBP, DBP, DIBP (4 phthalates)0.1% eachSome plastic components, packaging

RoHS Impact on CNC Material Selection

RoHS significantly affects material choices for CNC machined parts:

  • Leaded brass is restricted: Traditional free-machining brass (C36000) contains ~3% lead. For RoHS-compliant applications, switch to lead-free brass alloys like C27450 (EcoMachinable brass) or C69300. These are harder to machine and more expensive, but meet RoHS requirements.
  • 12L14 steel is out: This popular free-machining steel contains lead. Replace with 1215 (unleaded) or 1018 carbon steel for RoHS compliance.
  • Surface treatments matter: Hexavalent chromium conversion coatings (yellow chromate) are restricted. Use trivalent chromium alternatives or non-chromate passivation.
  • Exemptions exist: Some applications have specific exemptions — for example, copper alloys with up to 4% lead are exempt for certain connector applications (Annex III, exemption 6c). Always verify current exemption status.

REACH Compliance

What Is REACH?

REACH (Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals) is an EU regulation that addresses the production and use of chemical substances. Unlike RoHS, which targets specific substances in EEE, REACH is much broader — covering all chemicals manufactured or imported into the EU above 1 tonne per year.

REACH and CNC Machined Parts

For CNC parts, REACH primarily applies through:

  • Substances of Very High Concern (SVHC): REACH maintains a "Candidate List" of SVHCs (currently 200+ substances). If your part contains any SVHC above 0.1% by weight, you must communicate this information down the supply chain.
  • REACH Annex XVII restrictions: Specific restrictions on substances in articles — for example, nickel release from parts in prolonged skin contact.
  • Lead in metals: While RoHS exempts lead in alloys for machining, REACH may impose additional restrictions on lead content in articles depending on the application.

REACH vs. RoHS: Key Differences

  • Scope: RoHS = electrical/electronic equipment only; REACH = all products containing chemicals
  • Substance list: RoHS = 10 defined substances; REACH = 200+ SVHCs (and growing)
  • Threshold: Both use 0.1% (except cadmium at 0.01% for RoHS)
  • Obligation: RoHS = prohibition; REACH = communication obligation (must inform if SVHC > 0.1%) with some substance-specific restrictions

Conflict Minerals Compliance

The Dodd-Frank Act Section 1502 (US) and EU Conflict Minerals Regulation require companies to investigate whether their products contain tin, tantalum, tungsten, or gold (3TG) originating from conflict-affected regions, primarily the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) and adjoining countries.

For CNC parts, this primarily affects:

  • Tin: Used in solder and tin plating
  • Tungsten: Present in tungsten carbide tooling (less relevant to the finished part itself, but some customers audit the full manufacturing process)
  • Tantalum: Used in some electronic components that may be part of assemblies
  • Gold: Used in gold plating for connectors

Suppliers are expected to complete Conflict Minerals Reporting Templates (CMRT) developed by the Responsible Minerals Initiative (RMI). Most reputable CNC manufacturers maintain updated CMRT forms for their customers.

Industry-Specific Certification Requirements

Medical Devices

  • Biocompatibility testing (ISO 10993) for implantable or patient-contact parts
  • ISO 13485 quality management system
  • Full lot traceability from raw material to finished part
  • Material certificates: EN 10204 3.1 minimum, often 3.2 for implants
  • See our guide on CNC machining for medical device components

Aerospace

  • AMS (Aerospace Material Specifications) for material procurement
  • AS9100 quality management system
  • NADCAP accreditation for special processes (heat treatment, plating, NDT)
  • First Article Inspection Reports (FAIR) per AS9102
  • Material certifications: Must reference specific AMS specifications with traceable test data

Automotive

  • IATF 16949 quality management system
  • PPAP (Production Part Approval Process) documentation including material certifications
  • IMDS (International Material Data System) declarations for all materials in the vehicle
  • Substance restrictions: GADSL (Global Automotive Declarable Substance List)

Food Contact

  • FDA 21 CFR (US) or EU Regulation 1935/2004 for food-contact materials
  • Specific migration limits for metals — nickel, chromium, lead release testing
  • Surface finish requirements — typically Ra 0.8 µm or better for cleanability. See our surface finish guide

How to Request Material Certifications from Your CNC Supplier

Getting the right certifications starts with clear communication at the quoting stage. Here's a practical approach:

1. Specify in Your RFQ

Include certification requirements directly on your drawing or purchase order. For example:

  • "Material: AISI 316L per ASTM A276. Provide EN 10204 3.1 certificate."
  • "All materials must be RoHS 3 compliant. Provide compliance declaration."
  • "Heat number traceability required. Mark heat number on each part."

2. Clarify Costs Upfront

Most certifications don't add significant cost when specified upfront. However, requesting certifications after material has been ordered may be impossible (if the mill didn't retain samples for testing). The cost of certification is typically:

  • MTR (3.1): Usually included at no extra cost by reputable suppliers
  • RoHS/REACH declarations: Free (self-declaration) or $50–$200 per material for third-party testing
  • Third-party material testing: $100–$500 per test (XRF analysis, chemical analysis)

3. Verify, Don't Just File

Receiving a certificate is step one. Verifying it is equally important:

  • Check that chemical composition falls within the alloy specification limits
  • Verify heat numbers match between the MTR and your parts
  • Confirm the issuing mill is a recognized, legitimate material supplier
  • For critical applications, consider independent third-party verification testing

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a Mill Test Report (MTR)?

A Mill Test Report (MTR), also called a Material Test Report or certified mill test report (CMTR), is a document from the material manufacturer that certifies the chemical composition and mechanical properties of a specific heat or lot of metal. It proves the material meets the specified alloy standard (e.g., ASTM A276 for stainless steel bar).

Do CNC parts need RoHS compliance?

If your CNC parts are used in electrical or electronic equipment sold in the EU, they must comply with the RoHS directive, which restricts lead, mercury, cadmium, hexavalent chromium, PBB, and PBDE. Even non-EU markets increasingly require RoHS compliance as a procurement standard.

What is the difference between RoHS and REACH?

RoHS restricts specific hazardous substances in electrical/electronic equipment (6 substances + 4 phthalates). REACH is broader — it regulates all chemicals manufactured or imported into the EU above 1 ton/year and maintains a growing Substances of Very High Concern (SVHC) candidate list. A part can be RoHS compliant but still have REACH obligations.

Should I request material certifications for every CNC order?

For critical applications (medical, aerospace, automotive safety), always request MTRs and relevant compliance certificates. For general commercial parts, requesting certifications on the first order and periodically thereafter is good practice. The cost of obtaining certifications is minimal compared to the risk of using non-conforming material.

What is EN 10204 3.1 certification?

EN 10204 3.1 is a European standard for material inspection documents. A 3.1 certificate is issued by the material manufacturer's quality department (independent of production) and certifies that the material meets the specified requirements based on specific inspection and testing of the delivered lot. It is the most commonly requested certification level for precision CNC parts.
