How to Write an Effective RFQ for CNC Machining Services

February 27, 2026 · 11 min read
← Back to Blog

The quality of your Request for Quote (RFQ) directly determines the quality of the quotes you receive. A vague or incomplete RFQ leads to inflated pricing, misunderstood requirements, and wasted time for both you and the supplier. A well-structured RFQ, on the other hand, gets you accurate pricing, realistic lead times, and confident suppliers who understand exactly what you need.

Whether you're sourcing CNC turned parts, milled components, or complex Swiss-type machined pieces, this guide will show you exactly what to include in your RFQ to get the best possible response from machine shops — especially when working with international suppliers in Taiwan or other Asian manufacturing hubs.

Why Your RFQ Matters More Than You Think

Most buyers underestimate how much their RFQ quality affects the quote they receive. Here's the reality: when a machine shop receives an incomplete RFQ, they have two choices — ask clarifying questions (which delays everything) or add a "risk premium" to their pricing to cover unknowns.

A survey of CNC shop owners found that 60-70% of initial RFQs are missing critical information. The shops that receive better-structured RFQs consistently provide more competitive pricing because they can accurately estimate costs without padding for uncertainty.

Think of it this way: you're not just asking for a price — you're giving the supplier enough information to make a confident commitment. The more confidence they have in understanding your requirements, the tighter they can price the job.

The 10 Essential Elements of a CNC Machining RFQ

1. Technical Drawings (2D and/or 3D)

This is the single most important element. Your drawing is the legal document that defines what you're buying. At minimum, include:

  • 2D engineering drawing (PDF) — with all dimensions, tolerances, and GD&T callouts clearly marked
  • 3D CAD model (STEP or IGES format) — for programming and visualization
  • Drawing revision number and date — to avoid quoting obsolete designs

If you don't have formal engineering drawings, at the very least provide a dimensioned sketch with critical tolerances called out. But be aware that informal sketches often lead to misinterpretation — especially across language barriers. For guidance on specifying tolerances correctly, see our CNC machining tolerance guide.

2. Material Specification

Don't just say "stainless steel" or "aluminum." Specify the exact grade:

  • ✅ "SUS 303 (ASTM A582)" or "6061-T6 aluminum"
  • ❌ "stainless steel" or "some kind of aluminum"

If you need a material certification (MTR/mill cert), state this explicitly. Also mention if you have flexibility on material grade — this gives the supplier room to suggest a more cost-effective or readily available alternative.

3. Quantity and Lot Sizes

Pricing varies dramatically with quantity. Always specify:

  • Initial order quantity (e.g., 500 pcs)
  • Estimated annual usage (e.g., 5,000 pcs/year)
  • Request pricing at multiple quantities (e.g., quote for 100 / 500 / 1,000 / 5,000 pcs)

This helps the supplier optimize their quoting — they may use different tooling strategies, batch approaches, or machine selections depending on volume. For more on how quantity affects pricing, read our CNC machining cost breakdown.

4. Tolerances and Critical Dimensions

Clearly distinguish between:

  • Critical dimensions — tight tolerances that must be held (e.g., ±0.01mm)
  • General tolerances — standard machining tolerances (e.g., ±0.05mm per ISO 2768-m)
  • Reference dimensions — non-critical, for information only

Over-tolerancing is the #1 cost driver in CNC machining. If every dimension on your drawing has ±0.01mm tolerance, either you genuinely need that (expensive) or your drawing doesn't reflect actual functional requirements. Good suppliers will ask about this — great ones will suggest where tolerances can be relaxed to save cost.

5. Surface Finish Requirements

Specify the required surface finish using Ra values:

  • Ra 3.2 µm — standard machined finish (most economical)
  • Ra 1.6 µm — fine machined finish
  • Ra 0.8 µm — very fine finish (may require grinding)
  • Ra 0.4 µm — mirror-like finish (grinding or polishing required)

Also specify any post-machining treatments: anodizing, plating, heat treatment, passivation, etc. These significantly affect cost and lead time.

6. Quality and Inspection Requirements

Define what quality documentation you need:

  • First Article Inspection Report (FAIR) — typically AS9102 format for aerospace
  • Dimensional inspection report — for critical dimensions
  • Material certifications — MTR, RoHS, REACH compliance
  • Certificate of Conformance (CoC)

If you require specific inspection methods (e.g., CMM measurement, optical inspection), state this upfront. Some shops charge extra for comprehensive inspection reports, and it's better to know this before committing.

7. Delivery Requirements

Be specific about:

  • Required delivery date or lead time expectation
  • Shipping method — air freight, sea freight, express courier
  • Delivery terms — FOB, CIF, DDP, etc.
  • Packaging requirements — especially for delicate or high-precision parts

8. Industry Standards and Certifications

If your parts need to comply with specific industry standards, list them:

  • ISO 9001 quality management
  • IATF 16949 (automotive)
  • AS9100 (aerospace)
  • ISO 13485 (medical devices)
  • RoHS / REACH compliance

9. Commercial Terms

Include your expectations for:

  • Payment terms — Net 30, 50% deposit, etc.
  • Incoterms — who pays for shipping and insurance
  • Quote validity period — how long should the quote be valid
  • NDA requirement — if the design is confidential

10. Contact Information and Timeline

This seems obvious, but many RFQs lack:

  • Name and title of the decision-maker
  • Preferred communication method (email, phone, WeChat, LINE)
  • Quote deadline — when you need the quote back by
  • Decision timeline — when you plan to award the order

Common RFQ Mistakes That Cost You Money

Mistake #1: Sending Only a 3D Model Without Drawings

A 3D model shows geometry but doesn't communicate tolerances, surface finish, thread specifications, or material requirements. Without a 2D drawing, the supplier has to guess — and they'll guess conservatively (expensive).

Mistake #2: Not Specifying General Tolerances

If your drawing doesn't call out a general tolerance standard (like ISO 2768-m), the supplier doesn't know what precision level to target for un-dimensioned features. This leads to inconsistent quoting between suppliers.

Mistake #3: Requesting Quotes at a Single Quantity

Always ask for pricing at 2-3 quantity levels. This reveals the setup cost vs. per-piece cost structure and helps you plan economically. A part that costs $15 each at 100 pieces might drop to $4 each at 5,000 pieces.

Mistake #4: Ignoring Secondary Operations

Heat treatment, plating, anodizing, laser marking, assembly — if these are needed, include them in the RFQ. Finding out after the fact that your supplier doesn't offer anodizing means extra logistics, cost, and lead time.

Mistake #5: Being Vague About Timeline

"ASAP" is not a delivery requirement. Specify actual dates. Suppliers prioritize jobs with clear, firm deadlines. Vague timelines get vague priority.

How to Send Your RFQ for Maximum Response

Format Your Files Properly

  • Name files clearly: PartNumber_RevB_Drawing.pdf, PartNumber_RevB.step
  • Combine related documents into a single ZIP if sending multiple parts
  • Use PDF for drawings (not native CAD formats that the supplier may not have software for)
  • Keep file sizes reasonable — compress large 3D models if needed

Write a Clear Cover Email

Your cover email should summarize the key points without requiring the supplier to open every attachment just to understand the scope. Include: number of parts, materials, approximate quantities, and timeline. This helps the supplier's sales team route your RFQ to the right engineer quickly.

Send to Multiple Suppliers — But Not Too Many

A good practice is to send your RFQ to 3-5 qualified suppliers. Fewer than 3 and you lack comparison data. More than 5 and you're creating unnecessary work for yourself (and the suppliers). Evaluating Quotes: What to Compare Beyond Price

When quotes come back, resist the urge to simply pick the lowest number. Compare:

  • Price per piece — at each quoted quantity
  • Tooling costs — one-time NRE (Non-Recurring Engineering) charges
  • Lead time — production time plus shipping
  • What's included — inspection reports, packaging, certifications
  • What's excluded — shipping, customs, secondary operations
  • Payment terms — 100% upfront vs. Net 30 has real cost implications

Special Considerations for International RFQs

When sending RFQs to overseas CNC suppliers — particularly in Taiwan, China, or other Asian manufacturing countries — keep these additional points in mind:

  • Use metric units — or clearly specify if dimensions are in inches
  • Specify material standards — JIS, ASTM, DIN, or GB equivalents may differ
  • Account for time zones — set realistic response deadlines considering the time difference
  • Language clarity — avoid idioms and jargon. Simple, direct English works best
  • Currency — specify USD, EUR, or local currency for pricing
  • IP protection — include NDA requirements upfront if your design is proprietary

Taiwan-based suppliers like KING HAN generally operate to international standards (ISO, ASTM) and communicate fluently in English, which significantly reduces the friction common with some other Asian sourcing destinations.

Sample RFQ Email Template

Here's a framework you can adapt:

Subject: RFQ — [Part Name/Number] — [Quantity] pcs — [Your Company]

Dear [Supplier],

We are looking for a CNC machining partner for the following part(s):

Part: [Name/Number, Rev]
Material: [Grade, e.g., SUS 304]
Quantity: Please quote [100 / 500 / 2,000] pcs
Annual Usage: ~[X] pcs/year
Tolerance: General per ISO 2768-m; critical dimensions per drawing
Surface Finish: [Ra value] + [any treatment]
Delivery: Need by [date]; [FOB/CIF] to [location]
Quality: Dimensional report required; material cert required

Please find attached: 2D drawing (PDF), 3D model (STEP).

Kindly reply by [date] with unit pricing, tooling cost, and lead time.

[Your Name, Title, Company, Contact]

Key Takeaways

  • A well-prepared RFQ saves time and gets you more accurate, competitive quotes
  • Always include 2D drawings with tolerances — 3D models alone are not enough
  • Specify material grade, surface finish, and quality requirements explicitly
  • Request pricing at multiple quantity levels to understand cost structure
  • Compare quotes holistically — not just on unit price
  • For international sourcing, be explicit about units, standards, and commercial terms

The effort you put into your RFQ directly translates to the quality of quotes — and ultimately, the quality of parts — you receive. Take the extra 30 minutes to prepare a thorough RFQ, and it will pay dividends throughout the entire project lifecycle.
