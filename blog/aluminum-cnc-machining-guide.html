Aluminum CNC Machining: Complete Alloy Selection Guide for Precision Parts

February 22, 2026 · 10 min read
← Back to Blog

Aluminum is the most popular material in CNC machining — and for good reason. It's lightweight, corrosion-resistant, highly machinable, and available in dozens of alloy grades, each engineered for specific performance requirements. But this abundance of options creates a common challenge: which aluminum alloy should you choose for your CNC machined parts?

The wrong alloy choice can mean parts that don't meet strength requirements, poor anodizing results, wasted material costs, or unnecessary machining difficulty. This guide covers the most commonly CNC-machined aluminum alloys, their properties, ideal applications, and how to select the right one for your project.

Understanding Aluminum Alloy Designations

Aluminum alloys are classified by a four-digit numbering system. The first digit indicates the primary alloying element, which determines the alloy family's fundamental characteristics:

SeriesPrimary AlloyKey Characteristics
1xxxPure aluminum (99%+)Excellent conductivity, very soft, limited machining use
2xxxCopperHigh strength, aerospace grade, poor corrosion resistance
3xxxManganeseModerate strength, good formability, sheet metal
5xxxMagnesiumMarine grade, good weldability, moderate strength
6xxxMagnesium + SiliconVersatile, good machinability, excellent anodizing
7xxxZincHighest strength, aerospace applications

The temper designation after the alloy number (e.g., "-T6") indicates the heat treatment condition. T6 means solution heat treated and artificially aged — the most common temper for CNC machined parts, offering the best combination of strength and machinability.

The Top Aluminum Alloys for CNC Machining

6061-T6 — The Universal Choice

If there's one aluminum alloy every CNC machinist knows, it's 6061-T6. It's the default choice for a reason — it does everything well and nothing poorly.

Key Properties:

  • Tensile strength: 310 MPa (45 ksi)
  • Yield strength: 276 MPa (40 ksi)
  • Hardness: 95 Brinell
  • Machinability: Very good
  • Weldability: Excellent
  • Corrosion resistance: Good (excellent when anodized)
  • Anodizing response: Excellent — produces uniform, attractive finishes

Best for: General-purpose structural components, brackets, housings, fixtures, jigs, frames, consumer electronics enclosures, drone parts, and any application where a balance of strength, machinability, and surface treatment compatibility is needed.

Limitations: Not suitable for high-stress aerospace structures or applications requiring strength above 350 MPa. In these cases, consider 7075.

7075-T6 — Maximum Strength

7075 is one of the strongest aluminum alloys available, approaching the strength of many steels while remaining one-third the weight. It's the go-to choice when strength-to-weight ratio is the primary design driver.

Key Properties:

  • Tensile strength: 572 MPa (83 ksi)
  • Yield strength: 503 MPa (73 ksi)
  • Hardness: 150 Brinell
  • Machinability: Good (slightly more tool wear than 6061)
  • Weldability: Poor (not recommended for welded assemblies)
  • Corrosion resistance: Moderate (lower than 6061, needs protection)
  • Anodizing response: Good, but produces a slightly different color than 6061

Best for: Aerospace structural components, high-performance automotive parts, sporting equipment, firearms components, military hardware, and any application where maximum strength with minimum weight is critical.

Limitations: More expensive than 6061 (typically 2–3×). Cannot be welded without significant strength loss. Less corrosion-resistant — protective surface treatments are recommended.

2024-T3/T4 — Aerospace Heritage

2024 has been used in aircraft structures since the 1930s. It offers excellent fatigue resistance — critical for parts subjected to cyclic loading.

Key Properties:

  • Tensile strength: 469 MPa (68 ksi)
  • Yield strength: 324 MPa (47 ksi)
  • Fatigue strength: Superior to most aluminum alloys
  • Machinability: Good
  • Corrosion resistance: Poor (requires cladding or anodizing)

Best for: Aircraft fuselage skins, wing structures, truck wheels, scientific instruments, and parts subject to cyclic or fatigue loading.

5052-H32 — Marine and Sheet Metal Grade

5052 offers the best corrosion resistance among common aluminum alloys, making it the default choice for marine environments and chemical exposure.

Key Properties:

  • Tensile strength: 228 MPa (33 ksi)
  • Corrosion resistance: Excellent, especially in saltwater
  • Weldability: Excellent
  • Machinability: Fair (softer and gummier than 6061)

Best for: Marine hardware, chemical processing equipment, fuel tanks, hydraulic tubes. Less commonly CNC machined due to lower machinability — better suited for sheet metal fabrication.

6082-T6 — The European Alternative to 6061

6082 is essentially Europe's answer to 6061, with slightly higher strength and very similar machining characteristics. Common in European specifications and increasingly used in Asian manufacturing.

Key Properties:

  • Tensile strength: 340 MPa (49 ksi) — slightly higher than 6061
  • Machinability: Very good
  • Corrosion resistance: Good
  • Anodizing response: Excellent

Best for: Structural components, bridges, cranes, transport applications. Direct substitute for 6061 in European-spec projects.

2011-T3 — The Free-Machining Champion

2011 is specifically formulated for maximum machinability. It contains lead and bismuth additions that produce small, easily broken chips and allow higher cutting speeds than any other aluminum alloy.

Key Properties:

  • Machinability: Excellent — rated 90% (brass = 100%)
  • Tensile strength: 379 MPa (55 ksi)
  • Corrosion resistance: Poor
  • Anodizing: Poor (lead content causes uneven finish)

Best for: High-volume screw machine parts, complex turned components, fittings, and connector bodies where surface treatment isn't required. Excellent on Swiss-type lathes for high-volume production.

Note: 2011 contains lead and is not suitable for food contact or medical applications. RoHS-compliant alternatives like 6262 are available.

Machining Aluminum: Best Practices

Aluminum's excellent machinability means fast cycle times and long tool life, but there are still important considerations for optimal results:

Cutting Parameters

  • Cutting speed: 200–600 m/min for most alloys (much faster than steel). Modern carbide tooling can push above 1,000 m/min on free-machining grades.
  • Feed rate: Aggressive feeds are possible — aluminum responds well to "heavy and fast" cutting strategies.
  • Depth of cut: Large depths of cut are typically fine, as aluminum generates relatively low cutting forces.

Tooling Considerations

  • Tool material: Uncoated carbide or PCD (polycrystalline diamond) tools perform best. TiAlN-coated tools designed for steel can cause built-up edge in aluminum.
  • Geometry: Sharp, polished cutting edges with large rake angles (15–20°) and wide chip-breaking grooves are ideal.
  • Coolant: Essential for preventing built-up edge (BUE) and achieving good surface finish. Water-soluble coolant or mist application works well.

Common Machining Challenges

  • Built-up edge: Softer alloys (1xxx, 3xxx, 5xxx) tend to weld onto cutting edges. Higher speeds, sharp tools, and adequate coolant mitigate this.
  • Burring: Aluminum tends to form burrs on exit edges. Proper tool geometry and deburring operations are standard practice.
  • Thin wall distortion: Aluminum's relatively low elastic modulus means thin-walled parts can distort under clamping or cutting forces. Specialized fixturing may be needed.
  • Long stringy chips: Some alloys (especially 6061) produce long, continuous chips that can wrap around tooling. Chip-breaking geometry and proper parameters help.

Surface Treatments for Aluminum CNC Parts

One of aluminum's greatest advantages is the variety of surface treatments available:

Anodizing

  • Type II (decorative): 5–25 µm oxide layer. Provides corrosion resistance and can be dyed in various colors (black, blue, red, gold, etc.). The most common finish for consumer products.
  • Type III (hard anodize): 25–75 µm oxide layer. Significantly increases surface hardness (60–70 HRC equivalent) and wear resistance. Ideal for functional parts. Note that hard anodizing adds dimensional buildup of approximately 50% of the total coating thickness per side.

Other Surface Treatments

  • Chromate conversion (Alodine/Iridite): Provides corrosion protection and a paint-adhesion base. Gold or clear color. Common for aerospace components.
  • Powder coating: Thick, durable paint finish for non-precision surfaces. Excellent UV and corrosion resistance.
  • Nickel plating: Adds wear resistance and a silver-metallic appearance. Electroless nickel provides very uniform coating thickness.
  • Bead blasting: Creates a uniform matte texture, often used before anodizing for a satin appearance.

When designing parts for anodizing, keep in mind that the anodize layer builds both inward and outward from the original surface. Critical tolerance features should either be masked during anodizing or dimensioned to account for the coating thickness.

How to Choose: A Decision Framework

Use this simple decision tree to select the right aluminum alloy:

  1. Need maximum strength? → 7075-T6 (no welding needed) or 2024-T3 (fatigue resistance)
  2. Need good all-around performance? → 6061-T6 (the safe default)
  3. Need excellent anodizing finish? → 6061-T6 or 6082-T6
  4. Need maximum corrosion resistance? → 5052 or anodized 6061
  5. Making high-volume turned parts? → 2011-T3 (non-RoHS) or 6262 (RoHS compliant)
  6. Need weldability + strength? → 6061-T6
  7. Budget is the priority? → 6061-T6 (most available, most competitive pricing)

When in doubt, start with 6061-T6. It's the most widely stocked, most commonly machined, and most forgiving aluminum alloy. Only move to specialty grades when your application specifically demands properties that 6061 can't deliver.
